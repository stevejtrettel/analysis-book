## Chapter 8: Integration

### Narrative

This chapter develops integration as an independent subject, before any connection to differentiation. Three simple axioms — rectangles, monotonicity, additivity — force the value of the integral for continuous functions. The Darboux construction verifies that these forced values actually define an integral satisfying the axioms.

With integration in hand, we return to the geometric definitions from §1.5 and prove the integral computes what the supremum defines. Volume, harder to define geometrically, is defined directly via integration.

The chapter closes with direct computations of powers and exponentials, showing that integration from first principles is possible but laborious.

### Historical Prelude

The chapter opens with an unnumbered historical introduction: "Integration Before Calculus"

- Archimedes and the method of exhaustion: trapping areas between inscribed and circumscribed figures
- Cavalieri's indivisibles: areas as sums of lines, volumes as stacks of slices
- Fermat's integration of powers via geometric partitions, forty years before Newton
- The message: integration existed for two millennia before calculus
- Our approach: axiomatic — what must the integral be, before we ask what it is?
