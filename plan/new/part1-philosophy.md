Status: discussion capture, adjudicated 2026-08-18. Steve approved the

## Why Part I is a part

Part I is one complete performance of the thesis, for the concept of
**number**. Practice: millennia of computing numbers by infinite
processes (Babylon's iteration, Archimedes' polygons, Napier's tables,
decimal arithmetic). Deferred question: what *is* the thing these
processes reach? Reckoning: the completeness axiom and the certification
toolkit it forces (MCT, Bolzano–Weierstrass, Cauchy — the five faces).
Doors: uncountability, indescribable numbers, transcendentals.

**Part I finishes number.** (Adjudicated: essentially correct.) Some
threads return in Part III — Cantor's construction, completion of ℚ as a
metric-space triviality in 11.5 — but as *revisits from a higher
vantage*, not unfinished business. Part II performs the same drama for
*function*; Part III for *space of functions*. Three parts, one
template, three concepts.

## The boundary with Part II

**Primary cut (Steve's formulation): isolated values vs. functions.**
Part I thinks about *isolated values*, one at a time — each obtained by
its own process: a^√2 by a supremum, sin at a constructible angle by
chord geometry, e as a limit, log 2 by Briggs's roots. (Not merely
"tables": a^√2 is nobody's table entry, but it is still a single value,
individually produced.) The next part is when we really think of these
values *together, as a function* — a single object carrying all values
at once, about which collective questions (continuity, growth, area
under) first make sense. Policy sentence for the chapter plans: **the
moment an object is treated as a function — limits of it, continuity of
it — we are in Part II.**

Reinforcing cuts:

- *Where infinity lives*: in Part I, in the index of a process — one
  limit, one quantifier-game at a time. In Part II, in the domain — 
  uncountably many values at once, and the signature theorems (IVT,
  EVT, MVT) are local-to-global assemblies, a different move from
  following one process to its end.
- *Fresh ground pedagogy*: students arrive with three years of function
  intuitions, some wrong, all emotionally held — and almost no
  experience with sequences. Part I trains the ε-grammar where there
  are no habits to unlearn; Part II then confronts their calculus with
  the grammar already secure. Numbers-first is tactically kind, not
  just logically clean.
- *Historical traditions*: roughly, the computational/arithmetic
  tradition (Part I) vs. the calculus tradition (Part II).

## Where students enter

As practicing Eulers who don't know what a number is: fluent in
computation, trusting infinite processes since age ten (1/3 = 0.333…,
π ≈ 3.14159, long division) without noticing that the most familiar
objects they own are already infinite processes in disguise. Part I's
opening move is not "new hard material" but "here is what you have been
standing on" — the student's position is the historical position of
1800. (Preface conceit: this course is their 19th century.)

## What students leave with — the exit contract

1. **The toolkit** (what Part II formally requires): sup/inf fluency;
   ε-N and the limit laws; the certification theorems and the skill of
   proving convergence *without naming the limit*; series machinery
   through dominated convergence.
2. **The case studies**: √2, π, e — each carried from ancient
   computation to certified object; the three constants genuinely
   theirs.
3. **The meta-lessons** (the real exports): the axiomatic method seen
   twice (ℝ; measurement); deferral-and-reckoning experienced in
   miniature (Ch 1's named IOUs, some redeemed within Part I, some
   visibly outstanding); and the discipline — infinity obeys rules, and
   the rules are learnable, not folklore.
