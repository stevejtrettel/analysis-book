# The "Unspeakable Numbers" Etymology — Sources and Caveats

Sourcing note for the Ch 1 doctrine-etymology material (and possible
title use). Compiled 2026-08-18 from web-verifiable sources; items
marked ⚠ VERIFY need a library check against print editions before
anything is quoted in the book.

## The words (Ancient Greek, both)

- **λόγος** (*logos*): word, speech, account, reckoning — and
  *ratio*. The double meaning is the whole point. [LSJ s.v. λόγος]
- **ἄλογος** (*alogos*): alpha-privative + λόγος — "without logos":
  speechless/wordless AND without-ratio/irrational. Euclid's technical
  term for irrational lines/areas in Elements Book X. Plural ἄλογοι.
  [LSJ s.v. ἄλογος; Euclid X Def. 3]
- **ῥητός** (*rhētos*): "stated, expressible, that may be spoken" —
  verbal adjective from the root of εἴρω/ἐρῶ "to speak" (same root as
  *rhetoric*). Euclid's term for rational lines: ῥητή (fem.),
  plural ῥηταί. [LSJ s.v. ῥητός]
- **ἄρρητος** (*arrhētos*): alpha-privative + ῥητός — "unspoken,
  unsayable"; in religious contexts "secret, not to be divulged" (of
  the mysteries); in mathematics, the OLDER term for irrational,
  attested in Plato. Note the spelling: double rho, ἄρρητος.
  [LSJ s.v. ἄρρητος]

## Primary attestations

1. **Plato, Republic 546c** — the nuptial-number passage speaks of
   ῥητῶν and ἀρρήτων "diameters of the pempad": the "rational
   diameter of five" is 7 (approximating √50), the ἄρρητος diameter
   is √50 itself. This is the classic early mathematical use of
   arrhētos = irrational. Confirmed via Perseus (Shorey translation +
   Adam's commentary ad loc.). ⚠ VERIFY exact Greek wording against
   Burnet's OCT before quoting Greek in print.
2. **Euclid, Elements Book X, Def. 3** — the technical pair: the
   assigned line and those commensurable with it are ῥηταί
   ("rational/expressible"), those incommensurable ἄλογοι
   ("irrational"). Confirmed via Joyce's edition (Clark U).
3. **Plato, Theaetetus 147d–148b** — Theodorus/Theaetetus on
   incommensurable δυνάμεις; uses a different vocabulary (δύναμις,
   σύμμετρος/ἀσύμμετρος), worth knowing it does NOT use
   alogos/arrhētos here.

## ⚠ THE TRAP — Euclid's technical usage is NOT ours

Euclid's Book X "rational" (ῥητή) includes lines **commensurable in
square only** with the assigned line. Consequence: **the diagonal of
the unit square is ῥητή — rational — in Euclid's technical sense**
(its square has ratio 2:1 to the unit square). So the sentence "Euclid
calls the diagonal *alogos*" is FALSE and must never appear.

Safe formulations:
- The *general* Greek mathematical vocabulary called incommensurable
  magnitudes ἄλογα / ἄρρητα — "without ratio/word," "unsayable" —
  and Plato's Republic uses ἄρρητος of √50 (the diagonal-of-five).
- The etymology point (nameless = the ancient framing) stands on
  λόγος's double meaning and on Plato's ἄρρητος, NOT on Euclid's
  technical classification.
- Heath's commentary (Elements vol. III, on X Def. 3) discusses the
  terminological history — ἄρρητος as the older word, ἄλογος as
  Euclid's technical choice. ⚠ VERIFY exact pages at the library.

## The Hippasus legend — use only AS legend

The drowning-for-disclosure story: late sources (Iamblichus; a
scholium tradition), and the "divine punishment for revealing the
ἄρρητον" framing. Modern scholarship: von Fritz, "The Discovery of
Incommensurability by Hippasus of Metapontum," Annals of Mathematics
46 (1945), reconstructs the discovery (pentagon/pentagram route);
Burkert, *Lore and Science in Ancient Pythagoreanism* (1972), is
skeptical of the secrecy-and-punishment story; current consensus
treats drowning/secrecy as legend, possibly fueled by the pun on
ἄρρητος (unsayable ↔ secret). If the book uses it, label it a legend
— the *pun* (the irrational as the literally-unspeakable-secret) is
the usable, honest part.
